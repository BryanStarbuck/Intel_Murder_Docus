Boeing quality-control manager who spent 32 years at the company and became its best-known whistleblower, warning that 787 Dreamliners were being built with faulty oxygen systems, metal shavings near flight-control wiring, and lost or defective parts. He was found shot dead in his truck in a Charleston hotel parking lot on the morning he was due for his third day of depositions in his case against Boeing. Police and the coroner ruled it a suicide.

**What the video says about this case (#3):** these are the narrator's claims, as stated in the video.

- Barnett spent 32 years in quality control at Boeing. At the 787 Dreamliner factory in North Charleston he found the emergency oxygen systems were not deploying. According to the video, **Boeing's own research unit tested 300 of them and 75 failed: one in four.**
- He reported it internally; according to the narrator, Boeing did not act, so he went to a regulator and sued Boeing for retaliating against him.
- In March 2024 he spent **two days** in Charleston giving sworn evidence against Boeing. On the third day he did not show up. He was found in his truck in the hotel car park, dead from a gunshot that was ruled self-inflicted.
- Two months later a second Boeing whistleblower, [Joshua Dean](Joshua_Dean.mdx), died as well.

## Assessment: MODERATE SUSPICION

Barnett died in the middle of giving sworn testimony against one of America's largest defense and aerospace contractors, and he had reportedly told a friend, "if anything happens to me, it's not suicide." That timing and that warning are why millions of people doubted the ruling. But the physical evidence is unusually strong: surveillance shows he arrived alone and no one approached his truck, the truck was locked with the key fob in his pocket, ballistics matched the gun in his hand, and a handwritten note carried only his fingerprints. His own family has not claimed he was murdered. They allege that years of harassment and retaliation by Boeing drove him to it, and sued Boeing for wrongful death on that basis. The open question in this case is less "who pulled the trigger" than what years of retaliation did to a man who told the truth about airplane safety.

## Who He Was

### Early Life

John Barnett was born in Mount Shasta, California, and raised in Louisiana, the youngest of four boys (he had three older brothers). His father was a rail worker.

After high school he enlisted in the U.S. Air Force. He then worked at Rockwell International on parts for the Space Shuttle and the B-1 Lancer bomber.

### 32 Years at Boeing

He joined Boeing around 1985 as a quality inspector, at first in the Everett, Washington factory that built the 747, 767, and 777. Over the years he moved up into lead positions and helped build Boeing's training programs.

From about 2010 to 2017 he was a **quality-control manager at Boeing South Carolina** in North Charleston, where the 787 Dreamliner was being built.

He retired in 2017, citing stress from the job and a doctor's warning that he was at risk of a heart attack. He lived in Pineville, Louisiana, near his mother.

### Personal Life

He was married to Cindy Swafford; they later divorced. He then married Diane Johnson, who died of brain cancer in 2022.

### In the Public Eye

He was featured in a 2019 *New York Times* article about production problems at the South Carolina plant, and in the 2022 Netflix documentary *Downfall: The Case Against Boeing*. He also spoke to the BBC.

## What He Saw at the South Carolina Plant

Barnett described a change in the culture at Boeing South Carolina:

- Heavy **pressure to hit schedule and cost targets**.
- **"Self-inspection" programs** in which mechanics checked their own work, cutting back on independent quality checks.
- **Hiring of less-experienced workers.**
- Pressure **not to fully document defects**.

He reported problems internally and filed ethics complaints. He said Boeing retaliated against him with poor performance reviews, undesirable shifts, demotions or reassignments, and blocked transfers.

## His Whistleblower Allegations

He raised these concerns with Boeing management, the FAA (Federal Aviation Administration), journalists including the BBC and the *New York Times*, and in his legal case:

### Emergency Oxygen That Might Not Work

He said he arranged tests on about 300 of the emergency oxygen systems used on 787s and found a high failure rate. He claimed that **roughly one in four would not work properly in an emergency**, meaning that if a cabin lost pressure, a quarter of passengers might not get oxygen.

Boeing acknowledged a supplier problem with some oxygen bottles but disputed how widespread it was, and said none of the faulty ones were installed on aircraft delivered to customers. The FAA was notified.

### Metal Shavings Near Flight-Control Wiring

He said **clusters of titanium or other metal shavings** were being left near critical electrical systems, including flight-control wiring. Sharp slivers of metal can cut through wire insulation over time, which he warned could cause a catastrophic failure.

In 2017 the FAA directed Boeing to take remedial action and remove the shavings before planes were delivered.

### Lost and Defective Parts

- **Missing or untracked "non-conforming" parts** (parts that failed inspection and were supposed to be set aside). An FAA report noted at least 53 such parts.
- Allegations that **defective or scrap parts were sometimes fitted onto planes** under schedule pressure.
- **Incomplete tracking** of components.
- **Debris left inside aircraft**, for example a ladder found inside a horizontal stabilizer during a test flight.

### A Culture of Speed Over Safety

More broadly, he said the plant put production speed and profit ahead of quality, safety documentation, and proper procedure.

### What Was Confirmed and What Boeing Denies

Some of his reports were partly backed up by Boeing's own internal reviews or by FAA action, including on the oxygen systems, the non-conforming parts, and the metal shavings directive. Boeing has denied his broader claims that defective parts were deliberately installed or that safety was compromised on delivered aircraft.

## The Legal Case

In 2017 Barnett filed a whistleblower retaliation complaint with OSHA under AIR 21, the federal law that protects aviation-industry employees who report safety issues. OSHA investigated and closed the complaint in Boeing's favor. He appealed.

The case was still going when he died. Depositions, in which he gave sworn testimony and was questioned by Boeing's lawyers, were underway in Charleston, and a hearing or trial was possible in June 2024. His attorneys and family carried parts of the litigation on after his death.

## Circumstances of Death

Barnett was in Charleston, South Carolina, for depositions in his case against Boeing. On **March 9, 2024**, he did not show up for the **third day of questioning**.

A wellness check was requested. He was found dead in the driver's seat of his locked orange Dodge Ram truck in the Holiday Inn parking lot, with a **single gunshot wound to the head**. He was holding a legally owned Smith & Wesson pistol he had bought years earlier.

What investigators found:

- **A notebook on the passenger seat** with writing that amounted to a suicide note. Only his fingerprints were on it. It included lines such as **"I can't do this any longer"** and **"I pray Boeing pays,"** along with expressions of love for his family and friends and criticism of Boeing and of the whistleblower system.
- **Hotel surveillance** showed he arrived at the property alone the evening before and re-parked his truck. No one else approached or got into the vehicle. His brake lights blinked early the next morning.
- **The truck was locked**, with the key fob in his pocket.
- **No signs of forced entry or a struggle.**

### Official Findings

The Charleston County coroner first described the wound as apparently self-inflicted. After further investigation, including ballistics confirming the gun in his hand fired the fatal bullet, an autopsy, his medical records, interviews, and the surveillance footage, the Charleston Police and the coroner concluded in **May 2024** that the manner of death was **suicide**.

Investigators cited longstanding mental-health problems (anxiety, depression, and PTSD) that grew worse during the legal proceedings, the chronic stress of the whistleblower process, and his grief over his wife's death. The case was closed as a suicide.

## What His Family Says

Shortly after his death, his brother **Rodney** and other family members said Barnett had been deeply worried about aircraft safety. They described a **"culture of concealment"** at Boeing that put profits over safety, and said the PTSD and anxiety caused by a hostile work environment contributed to his death.

His lawyers said at first that he had seemed in good spirits and was looking forward to resolving the case.

In 2025 his mother, **Vicky Stokes**, and his brothers filed a **wrongful-death lawsuit against Boeing**. According to the lawsuit, Boeing carried out a years-long campaign of harassment, abuse, intimidation, poor reviews, and retaliation that foreseeably led to his severe mental-health decline and raised his risk of suicide. These are allegations in a civil suit. Boeing expressed sadness and offered condolences.

Some related claims were later resolved or settled. Public details are limited; one account notes a partial settlement of at least $50,000 in one related matter.

His brother has publicly called claims that he was assassinated **far-fetched**.

His story was told again in the Netflix documentary *Freefall: A Reckoning for Boeing*.

## Why This Death Raises Questions

- He died **in the middle of sworn depositions** in his case against Boeing, on the morning of his third day of questioning.
- He had reportedly told a friend privately: **"if anything happens to me, it's not suicide."**
- His lawyers said he had seemed in good spirits and was looking forward to resolving the case.
- His case had the potential to put Boeing's safety practices under oath and on the record, at a time when Boeing was under intense scrutiny over safety.
- **[Joshua Dean](Joshua_Dean.mdx)**, another whistleblower in Boeing's supply chain, represented by the same law firm, died of an infection only months later, in 2024. Some online commentators see a pattern; official accounts treat the two deaths as unrelated.
- More broadly, the case shows what happens to people who report safety problems: years of retaliation claims, a complaint closed in the company's favor, an appeal still unresolved after seven years.

## Counterarguments / Official Explanation

- Surveillance shows he arrived alone, and no one approached or entered his truck.
- The truck was locked with the key fob in his own pocket.
- Ballistics confirmed the gun in his hand fired the bullet, and he had owned that gun legally for years.
- The note was in his notebook, carried only his fingerprints, and expressed despair and love for his family.
- He had a documented history of anxiety, depression, and PTSD, worsened by the legal fight, and he was grieving his wife.
- His own family's lawsuit does not claim he was murdered. It claims Boeing's alleged harassment drove him to suicide.
- His brother has called assassination claims far-fetched.

Police, the coroner, and some family members have pointed to this evidence as inconsistent with a homicide.

## Public Reaction and Social Media

Theories spread quickly online claiming that Boeing ordered a hit or staged the death as a suicide. They cited the timing during depositions, his warning to a friend that "if anything happens to me, it's not suicide," and the high profile of the case. These theories were heavily amplified on social media. **No evidence has been found to support them, and Boeing has not been accused by any official body of involvement in his death.**

On X, posts often group Barnett with other high-profile whistleblower deaths, including [Suchir Balaji](Suchir_Balaji.mdx). They refer to the timing and the official suicide ruling and discuss Boeing's safety culture, retaliation claims, and the wider pattern of disputed deaths.

## Where the Case Stands

**Official position:** suicide. The police investigation is complete and closed.

**Family position:** suicide caused by years of alleged workplace harassment and retaliation; their litigation focuses on that rather than on a staged homicide.

**What is still debated:** not the forensic facts of the death, but how severe the alleged retaliation was, whether whistleblower protections work, and the human cost of years-long legal fights over safety.
