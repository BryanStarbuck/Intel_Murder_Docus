## Assessment: SUSPICIOUS

Swartz's death is officially ruled a suicide, and the case for prosecutorial overreach as a contributing cause is well-documented and uncontroversial. What lifts this beyond "tragic suicide under legal pressure" is the specific context: he died exactly two days after prosecutors rejected his defense team's counter-offer; JSTOR — the alleged victim — had already reached a settlement with him, asked prosecutors to drop charges, and announced four million articles would be made free two days before his death; and his former girlfriend was subpoenaed and pressured into providing information against him. The federal government assembled a 14,500-page file on Swartz — the vast majority still unreleased — far in excess of what any academic piracy case would require. The specific claim that his 2008 PACER bulk-download of 2.7 million federal court documents was the real trigger for his prosecution — not the JSTOR case — is unverified but documented in the pattern: the government began surveillance of Swartz in 2008 after his PACER project and his "Guerrilla Open Access Manifesto," years before the JSTOR incident.

## Circumstances of Death

On the evening of January 11, 2013, Aaron Swartz was found by his girlfriend, Taren Stinebrickner-Kauffman, hanging in his Brooklyn, New York apartment. He had used his own belt. He was 26 years old. No suicide note was found.

At the time of his death, Swartz faced:

- 2 counts of wire fraud
- 11 violations of the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA)
- Combined maximum sentence of **35 years in federal prison**
- $1 million in fines
- Mandatory asset forfeiture
- Supervised release

Two days before his death — on January 9, 2013 — federal prosecutors had rejected a counter-offer from Swartz's defense team. That same day, JSTOR announced it was making more than 4.5 million academic articles available to the public free of charge. JSTOR's own lawyer, former U.S. Attorney Mary Jo White, had already asked prosecutors to drop the charges. The alleged victim did not want the prosecution to proceed. The government pursued it anyway.

Stinebrickner-Kauffman later said she had not been worried about Swartz's state until the final 24 hours of his life.

## Background

### Prodigy Years

Aaron Swartz was one of the most technically accomplished young people in the history of the internet. His biography by age 26 includes:

- **Age 12**: Created *The Info Network*, a user-generated encyclopedia predating Wikipedia; won the ArsDigita Prize for educational, collaborative websites
- **Age 13**: Wrote the specification for RSS 1.0, the technology that powers podcasts and web feeds today
- **Age 14**: Co-authored the RSS 1.0 specification as a member of the W3C working group — working alongside adults decades older
- **Age 15**: Became a key contributor to the Creative Commons technical architecture
- **Age 19**: Co-owner of Reddit after the social news site merged with his company Infogami in 2006; departed in 2007
- Built web.py, a Python web framework still in active use
- Contributed to the Markdown syntax specification

By his early twenties, Swartz had already made a fortune from his Reddit work and was one of the most prominent voices for digital civil liberties and open access to publicly funded research.

### The Guerrilla Open Access Manifesto (2008)

In July 2008, Swartz wrote and published the "Guerrilla Open Access Manifesto" from Eremo, Italy. It opened:

> *"Information is power. But like all power, there are those who want to keep it for themselves. The world's entire scientific and cultural heritage, published over centuries in books and journals, is increasingly being digitized and locked up by a handful of private corporations."*

The manifesto called on readers to download academic papers, share them freely, and upload them to file-sharing networks. It was a direct call to civil disobedience in the name of information freedom. The Secret Service later confirmed that federal surveillance of Swartz began after the publication of this manifesto — years before the JSTOR incident.

### The PACER Project (2008)

In September 2008, Swartz worked with Carl Malamud's Public.Resource.Org on a project to bulk-download federal court documents from the PACER (Public Access to Court Electronic Records) system. PACER charges eight cents per page for access to public court records — government documents not covered by copyright.

Using free library trial access credentials provided by the Government Printing Office, Swartz downloaded approximately **2.7 million federal court documents — nearly 20 million pages** of text and made them freely available.

The FBI launched an investigation. Within two months, the investigation was closed without charges because the documents were, in fact, public records. But what Swartz's bulk download revealed was significant: **PACER records were full of privacy violations** — exposed Social Security numbers, names of confidential informants, medical records, and the identities of minor children in court cases. He had exposed that the federal court system was violating its own privacy rules at scale.

The documents he accessed included sealed case materials and materials containing sensitive information about ongoing investigations. The full scope of what he accessed has never been fully disclosed.

### Defeating SOPA (2012)

Swartz co-founded Demand Progress in 2010. In late 2011, he became the leading organizer of the grassroots campaign against the Stop Online Piracy Act (SOPA) and the PROTECT IP Act (PIPA) — legislation that would have allowed the Department of Justice to shut down entire internet domains based on alleged copyright infringement, without trial.

Swartz and Demand Progress built the campaign infrastructure, simplified the legislative language for ordinary people, and drove the mobilization that resulted in the largest online protest in history:

- On **January 18, 2012**, Wikipedia, Google, Reddit, Craigslist, Mozilla, and more than 50,000 websites blacked out or displayed protest banners
- Over **10 million petition signatures** were collected
- **Over 4 million emails** were sent to Congress
- **8 million phone calls** were made

SOPA and PIPA were shelved. After the victory, Swartz delivered the keynote at the F2C: Freedom to Connect conference in Washington, D.C. in May 2012, titled "How We Stopped SOPA."

He gave that speech while already under federal indictment.

### The JSTOR Case

Between September 2010 and January 2011, Swartz connected a laptop to MIT's network and downloaded approximately **4.8 million academic journal articles** from JSTOR — roughly 80% of JSTOR's entire database. JSTOR charges subscription fees for access to research that was largely funded by public grants.

JSTOR detected the bulk downloading, blocked the connection, and ultimately reached a **civil settlement with Swartz** in which the downloaded files were returned. JSTOR issued a statement explicitly stating it did not want criminal prosecution.

Federal prosecutors pursued the case anyway. U.S. Attorney **Carmen Ortiz** and Assistant U.S. Attorneys **Stephen Heymann** and **Scott Garland** brought the case. In September 2012 — after Swartz's SOPA victory — prosecutors **expanded** the charges from 4 counts to 13 counts, raising the maximum sentence to 35 years.

### The Plea Negotiations

Swartz's attorney Marty Weinberg later revealed that he had come close to negotiating a plea agreement in which Swartz would serve **no prison time**. "JSTOR signed off on it," Weinberg said. MIT refused.

Prosecutors ultimately offered six months in a low-security facility in exchange for guilty pleas on all 13 felony counts. Swartz and his attorneys declined, opting for trial. Two days before his death, prosecutors rejected the defense's counter-offer and the case headed toward trial. Swartz was found dead that evening.

## The Claimed Connection to Child Trafficking / Pedophile Ring Exposure

The specific claim that Swartz was killed because he had accessed sealed court records containing evidence of elite child abuse is found in alternative media and is based on the nature of his 2008 PACER project. The claim holds that among the 2.7 million federal court documents he downloaded, Swartz encountered sealed pedophile ring cases involving high-profile figures, and that this — not the JSTOR downloads — was the real trigger for the disproportionate prosecution that followed.

This claim is not documented in any official record, congressional investigation, or reporting by major news outlets.

However, several documented facts lend the PACER theory more weight than simple conspiracy:

1. **Federal surveillance began after PACER, not JSTOR.** The Secret Service opened a file on Swartz in 2008, the year of the PACER project and the Guerrilla Open Access Manifesto. The JSTOR incident occurred in 2010-2011. The government had been watching him for years before JSTOR.

2. **The PACER files contained sealed materials.** The privacy violations Swartz exposed included documents that should have been sealed or protected — Social Security numbers, confidential informant identities, names of minors. The scope of what else might have been in 20 million pages of federal court records has never been fully examined.

3. **14,500 pages of Secret Service files on Swartz remain mostly unreleased.** As of the time of reporting, only 104 of 14,500 pages had been released, most heavily redacted. No academic piracy case would generate this level of documentation.

4. **The prosecution was deliberately disproportionate.** Prosecutors charged Swartz with more felonies than the sentencing guidelines recommended after he defeated SOPA — the most powerful online censorship legislation in American history. The timing and escalation suggest a government that wanted Swartz specifically neutralized.

5. **The government pressured his girlfriend to testify against him.** Quinn Norton, Swartz's girlfriend from 2007 to 2011, was subpoenaed before a federal grand jury and entered a proffer agreement sharing information about his Guerrilla Open Access Manifesto. The relationship did not survive the strain.

The PACER-to-pedophile-records connection remains unverified. The documented injustice — prosecutorial overreach targeting a man who had defeated Congress's censorship agenda — is real and uncontested.

## Why This Death Raises Questions

- **The "victim" begged prosecutors to stop.** JSTOR settled with Swartz, declined to press charges, and had its own attorney ask federal prosecutors to drop the case. Prosecutors refused. When the alleged victim of a crime doesn't want a criminal prosecution, the question of why the government proceeded is legitimate.

- **Charges were expanded after SOPA, not before.** The original 2011 indictment carried 4 counts. After Swartz defeated SOPA in January 2012 — one of the most consequential acts of political organizing in internet history — prosecutors expanded to 13 counts in September 2012. The escalation followed the political victory.

- **Prosecutors rejected the last counter-offer two days before his death.** No agreement was possible. The government's position was that Swartz must plead guilty to multiple felonies and accept prison time. His family believes this legal entrapment drove him to suicide.

- **The Secret Service compiled 14,500 pages on an academic piracy suspect.** For comparison, the Secret Service typically builds files of this scale on threats to the President, major organized crime figures, and national security subjects. Not on a 26-year-old who downloaded journal articles.

- **Federal surveillance predated JSTOR by two years.** The government was building a case against Swartz from 2008 — after PACER and the Manifesto — not from 2010 when JSTOR occurred. JSTOR appears to have been the pretext rather than the cause.

- **MIT refused the no-jail plea deal that JSTOR had already approved.** MIT's position during prosecution was described as "neutral" in its own internal review. Yet MIT's refusal to sign off on a no-jail settlement was the deciding factor that prevented an agreement.

- **No suicide note.** Swartz left no documentation of his reasons.

- **The prosecution destroyed him financially.** Legal costs consumed his resources. He had sold his Reddit equity for a reported $1 million and spent much of it on the defense.

- **His girlfriend was subpoenaed and turned into a government informant.** The government's use of Quinn Norton — pressuring her through subpoena into cooperating — was a deliberate tactic to isolate Swartz and undermine his support network.

- **Lawrence Lessig, one of America's most prominent legal scholars, called it a "bully" prosecution.** This is not a fringe view. Congress opened hearings. Multiple law professors called the prosecution extraordinary.

## The Counterargument

Swartz had a documented history of depression dating back years. In a 2007 blog post, he wrote openly about depressive periods. Friends who knew him before his arrest had previously worried about his mental health and suggested counseling. The prosecution's crushing weight — a potential 35-year sentence at 26 — is more than sufficient as a proximate cause of suicide in a person with pre-existing depression.

Taren Stinebrickner-Kauffman, who found him, has attributed his death clearly to the prosecution's pressure — not to any external actor. She has never suggested anything other than suicide driven by legal despair.

The PACER-to-sealed-child-abuse-records claim is speculation found in alternative media with no documentary support. The FBI reviewed his PACER downloads in 2008 and closed the investigation. If he had discovered evidence of elite child abuse in those documents, there is no indication he ever attempted to disclose such material publicly.

The documented injustice in Aaron Swartz's death is the one his family, his partner, legal scholars, Congressmembers, and civil liberties organizations have consistently cited: federal prosecutors, in a case the victim didn't want, sought a punishment wildly disproportionate to the alleged harm.

## The MIT Report

MIT commissioned an internal review by Professor Hal Abelson, released in July 2013. Key findings:

- MIT never requested that federal charges be brought against Swartz
- MIT was not consulted about appropriate charges or punishment
- MIT maintained "neutrality" throughout the two-year prosecution
- But: MIT **refused to sign off** on a no-jail plea settlement that JSTOR had already approved

MIT's own review found no institutional wrongdoing, while simultaneously confirming the one institutional decision that made settlement impossible: refusing to consent to a deal that would have spared Swartz prison time.
